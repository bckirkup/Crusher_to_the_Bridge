# Clinical diagnostic accuracy at sea: evidence review

## Executive conclusions

Bedside findings alone rarely identify an infectious etiology reliably. Their principal shipboard value is to trigger isolation, exposure investigation, evacuation, and laboratory confirmation. Measles becomes clinically recognizable around the transition from a 2–4-day respiratory prodrome to rash; early Ebola virus disease (EVD) remains clinically inseparable from common febrile illnesses even during outbreaks; and hantavirus pulmonary syndrome (HPS) is nonspecific until abrupt cardiopulmonary deterioration. For Campylobacter, Legionella, and Vibrio gastroenteritis, no validated sensitivity and specificity for an unaided clinician’s pathogen-level impression were found. Where exact prelaboratory sensitivity/specificity has not been measured, it should be reported as **not established**, not inferred from symptom prevalence.

“Day 1,” “day 3,” and “day 5+” below refer to days after symptom onset, not exposure.

# 1. Measles

## Bedside evolution and earliest suspicion

The conventional illness begins with fever and a **2–4-day prodrome** of cough, coryza, and conjunctivitis, followed by a cephalocaudally spreading morbilliform rash. Consequently, on symptom day 1, “fever plus one of the three Cs” is sensitive as a respiratory-syndrome screen but extremely nonspecific. Suspicion can reasonably begin **before rash** when there is a compatible exposure, absent/two-dose-uncertain immunity, rising fever, all three respiratory features, and especially Koplik spots. Without an epidemiologic link or Koplik spots, prodromal measles cannot reliably be distinguished from influenza, COVID-19, adenovirus, or another viral upper-respiratory illness.

Koplik spots typically appear on the buccal mucosa opposite the molars **about 1–2 days before rash**, may be most evident around rash onset, and usually fade during the first several rash days. Reports commonly describe them in roughly **50–70%** of recognized cases, with wider study-to-study variation. Thus, their presence is highly suggestive in the correct context, but absence does not exclude measles. Neither a universal day-specific sensitivity nor a robust specificity estimate was recoverable from the retrieved comparative studies; “percent present” must not be equated automatically with sensitivity because examination timing and ascertainment differ.

## Accuracy of the classic clinical definition

The syndrome requested—generalized maculopapular rash, fever, and cough, coryza, or conjunctivitis—is essentially the WHO suspected-measles surveillance construct and overlaps the CDC clinical description. Published laboratory-comparison studies include Ferson et al., *Difficulties in clinical diagnosis of measles*; Hutchins et al., *Evaluation of the measles clinical case definition*; Brown et al., UK notified-case surveillance; and later outbreak/surveillance cohorts. Their central finding is consistent: the definition is intentionally sensitive but its specificity and positive predictive value deteriorate markedly where measles incidence is low, vaccination is common, and other febrile-rash illnesses predominate. The retrieved evidence did not support one universally valid WHO/CDC sensitivity and specificity pair; reporting a single value without population and outbreak context would be misleading.

The distinction between WHO and CDC usage is operationally important:

- **WHO suspected case:** fever, generalized maculopapular rash, and cough/coryza/conjunctivitis, or any case a clinician suspects.
- **CDC clinical description:** generalized maculopapular rash lasting ≥3 days, temperature ≥38.3°C, and cough/coryza/conjunctivitis. Confirmed-case classification ordinarily adds laboratory or epidemiologic evidence.

The ≥3-day rash requirement improves retrospective specificity but makes the CDC clinical description unsuitable as an early-isolation threshold. On a ship, isolation should begin when measles is suspected, not after three rash days.

## Time-dependent bedside performance

| Illness stage | What is observable | Practical accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Fever, malaise, early cough/coryza/conjunctivitis | Very low specificity; clinical measles may not yet satisfy a rash-based definition. |
| Days 2–3 | Intensifying fever/three Cs; Koplik spots may appear; exposure history becomes decisive | Sensitivity for clinical suspicion rises, but absence of Koplik spots cannot rule out disease. |
| Days 3–5 | Typical rash appears at hairline/face and spreads downward; fever often remains high | Classic definition becomes much more sensitive, but specificity remains prevalence-dependent. |
| Day 5+ | Confluent/fading rash, desquamation or complications | Retrospective pattern recognition improves; late or modified measles can remain atypical. |

## Differential diagnosis aboard ship

The principal mimics are rubella; parvovirus B19; roseola; enterovirus; adenovirus; influenza and COVID-19 with viral exanthem; dengue, chikungunya, and Zika after port exposure; scarlet fever; meningococcemia; secondary syphilis; acute HIV; rickettsial illness; and drug eruption. Varicella is usually vesicular rather than morbilliform. Important bedside discriminators are rash morphology and sequence, vaccination/exposure history, arthralgia or postauricular nodes suggesting rubella, “slapped cheek” appearance suggesting parvovirus, hypotension/petechiae suggesting invasive bacterial disease, and a temporally related new medication suggesting drug eruption.

## Maritime evidence

A systematic review identified 40 ship-associated measles cases, 69% in crew, and two ship outbreaks linked to **262 secondary cases ashore**. In a 2014 outbreak, 21/24 cases had negative or unknown vaccination histories; in a 2008 outbreak, 72% of cases were unvaccinated. Close crew quarters, multinational crew, and uncertain immunization records increase the pretest probability once one compatible case occurs.

Ship infirmaries often lack confirmatory capacity. In US maritime surveillance, a presumptive measles case triggered a contact investigation but was later laboratory-negative. This illustrates the expected tradeoff: early isolation has false positives, but delay can forfeit the narrow postexposure window—MMR within 72 hours or immune globulin within 6 days.

# 2. Ebola virus disease and viral hemorrhagic fevers

## Early clinical distinguishability

Early EVD is a nonspecific febrile systemic illness: fever, severe fatigue, headache, myalgia, anorexia, and sore throat. Vomiting and watery diarrhea often emerge over subsequent days. A rash, conjunctival injection, dysphagia, confusion, hiccups, and visible hemorrhage may increase suspicion, but none is sufficiently sensitive or specific alone. Exposure history—direct contact with a patient, corpse, blood/body fluid, unsafe funeral, infected animal, or affected healthcare facility—is therefore the most important early discriminator.

The West African WHO suspect criteria combined acute fever, contact history, or fever plus three symptoms such as headache, anorexia, lethargy, myalgia, breathing difficulty, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, dysphagia, or hiccups. These broad criteria selected many non-EVD patients. In one Sierra Leone treatment-center cohort, 158/566 (27.5%) admissions were PCR-positive, so **72.5% of clinically eligible admissions were false-positive suspects**. The higher-risk “probable” assessment identified 89% of positives, but 46% of that ward remained PCR-negative.

Healthcare-worker gestalt performed poorly: the subjective “Ebola look” was reported as approximately chance-level, and individual symptoms generally had low positive predictive value. This is recognition under outbreak conditions, where awareness and pretest probability were already high; performance in a non-outbreak shipboard setting should be expected to have an even lower positive predictive value.

## Hemorrhage: frequency and timing

Hemorrhage is neither an early nor obligatory feature, which is why “Ebola hemorrhagic fever” was replaced by EVD. When it occurs, it is generally a **later manifestation**, often around days 5–7 or thereafter in severe illness, and includes gingival bleeding, epistaxis, hematemesis, melena/hematochezia, hematuria, petechiae, purpura, or oozing from venipuncture sites. Waiting for bleeding substantially reduces sensitivity and delays isolation. The Sierra Leone study explicitly counted both visible bleeding and subcutaneous hemorrhage, but its more useful early predictors also included contact, conjunctivitis, diarrhea, fever, dysphagia, and referral at days 4–9.

For viral hemorrhagic fevers generally, the same caution applies. Lassa fever, Marburg disease, Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever, yellow fever, severe dengue, and Rift Valley fever often begin as nonspecific fever/myalgia; exposure geography, animal/vector contact, healthcare exposure, thrombocytopenia, jaundice, shock, or later bleeding changes suspicion but does not establish etiology.

## EVD versus malaria, typhoid, and influenza

- **Malaria:** fever, headache, weakness, vomiting, diarrhea, and myalgia overlap extensively. In the Sierra Leone cohort, malaria occurred in 35% and was twice as common among EVD-negative patients. Myalgia actually favored EVD-negative/malaria-positive status, whereas sick contact, conjunctivitis, diarrhea, dysphagia, hemorrhage, pyrexia, and presentation 4–9 days after onset favored EVD.
- **Typhoid:** progressive fever, headache, abdominal symptoms, diarrhea or constipation, and relative bradycardia overlap; neither rose spots nor abdominal findings are sufficiently sensitive.
- **Influenza/COVID-19:** cough and upper-respiratory symptoms favor a respiratory virus, but early EVD may include sore throat and systemic symptoms. Epidemiology dominates the distinction.

A 2017 weighted score discriminated EVD from EVD-negative and malaria-positive comparators with AUROC approximately 0.89–0.90, but this is discrimination, not 89% sensitivity and specificity.

A newer DRC score used asthenia, sore throat, conjunctivitis, bleeding gums, hematemesis, contact with a sick person, and contact with a traditional healer. At the selected threshold it achieved 81.4% sensitivity/53.6% specificity internally and 80.8%/41.4% externally. This quantifies the basic problem: even optimized outbreak screening sacrifices specificity to avoid missed cases.

## Time from onset to suspicion

A 4–9-day onset-to-referral interval was independently informative in the Sierra Leone score. This should not be interpreted as a biologic requirement; it reflects when illness became severe or recognizable and when patients reached an Ebola treatment center.

In an outbreak, contact tracing may trigger suspicion on day 1 of fever. Outside an outbreak, suspicion is commonly delayed until epidemiologic history is elicited, progressive gastrointestinal disease develops, or public-health consultation occurs. No robust comparative estimate of median onset-to-suspicion for outbreak versus non-outbreak shipboard practice was found.

## Time-dependent bedside performance

| Stage | Typical picture | Clinical accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Abrupt fever, fatigue, headache, myalgia, sore throat | Very high overlap with malaria, influenza, COVID-19, typhoid, dengue, and other infections. Exposure history supplies most specificity. |
| Day 3 | Persistent systemic illness; vomiting/diarrhea may begin | Sensitivity of broad suspect definitions rises; specificity remains low. |
| Day 5+ | Profuse GI loss, conjunctivitis, dysphagia, confusion, rash, shock; hemorrhage in a minority | Weighted clinical scores discriminate better, but laboratory confirmation remains mandatory. |

No ship-specific EVD accuracy study was found. Aboard ship, fever plus a credible high-risk exposure should trigger immediate separation, PPE, movement restriction, public-health notification, and evacuation planning; absence of bleeding must never reassure.

# 3. Hantavirus pulmonary/cardiopulmonary syndrome: Andes and other New World hantaviruses

## Timeline

HPS/HCPS generally has a **3–10-day prodrome** of fever, intense myalgia, headache, malaise, and often nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, or diarrhea. The cardiopulmonary phase then begins abruptly, with cough, dyspnea, tachypnea, hypoxemia, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, hemoconcentration, thrombocytopenia, and shock. Once deterioration begins, progression can be extraordinary: dyspnea to mechanical ventilation may occur within hours, and most deaths occur within 24–48 hours of hospital admission.

The wording in one retrieved review that cardiopulmonary disease begins “within 12–48 hours of prodromal onset” conflicts with the same source’s 3–10-day prodrome; the clinically coherent interpretation is that the **transition and subsequent deterioration occur over approximately 12–48 hours after the prodrome**, not that pulmonary disease routinely starts 12 hours after first fever.

## Clinical case definition and prediction rules

A purely bedside case definition has not been validated with reliable sensitivity and specificity. CDC surveillance definitions include fever, acute respiratory compromise/infiltrates or unexplained respiratory death, plus laboratory confirmation; they are classification rules rather than laboratory-free diagnostic tools.

The best-known rapid presumptive rule uses five CBC/smear findings:

1. thrombocytopenia;
2. hemoconcentration;
3. granulocytic left shift;
4. absence of toxic granulation; and
5. immunoblasts >10%.

Four of five findings yielded **89% sensitivity and 93% specificity** against serologically confirmed HCPS. This can be useful in a ship with a CBC and competent smear review, but it does not answer the accuracy of unaided bedside impression.

The retrieved Andes-virus cohort also found platelet count strongly predictive of progression among patients initially classified as mild, with AUROC 0.889; that is a severity rule, not an etiologic diagnostic rule. No validated no-laboratory Andes/HPS bedside score was identified.

## Distinction from other pneumonias

During the prodrome, HPS is clinically indistinguishable from influenza-like illness or gastroenteritis. Suspicion rises sharply when a previously healthy patient develops rapid dyspnea after a febrile myalgic prodrome, particularly with hypotension and a rodent exposure. Compared with ordinary bacterial pneumonia, early HPS often has fewer focal respiratory findings, little purulent sputum, abrupt bilateral edema, and disproportionate shock. Differential diagnoses include influenza/COVID-19, severe community-acquired pneumonia, leptospirosis, dengue, sepsis/ARDS, acute eosinophilic pneumonia, plague pneumonia, and cardiogenic pulmonary edema.

## Exposure history and ship relevance

The major clue is recent inhalational exposure to aerosolized rodent urine, feces, or saliva while sweeping or entering rodent-infested cabins, stores, warehouses, farms, sheds, or closed buildings. A ship is not intrinsically a classic HPS environment, but risk becomes plausible when rodents infest food stores/cargo spaces, crew clean enclosed contaminated compartments, or illness follows shore leave in an endemic rural area. For Andes virus, close person-to-person transmission has also been documented, so compatible illness in a close contact has additional importance.

In many patients, however, rodent contact is not recalled. Exposure history increases suspicion but has inadequate sensitivity to rule disease out. No maritime HPS diagnostic-accuracy cohort or reliable median onset-to-suspicion was found.

## Time-dependent performance

| Stage | Bedside assessment |
|---|---|
| Day 1 | Fever/myalgia/GI symptoms: essentially nonspecific; exposure history is the only meaningful discriminator. |
| Day 3 | Continued prodrome; thrombocytopenia/hemoconcentration may emerge if basic onboard testing exists. Purely clinical specificity remains low. |
| Day 5+ | Abrupt cough/dyspnea/hypoxemia/shock after prodrome strongly raises suspicion, but at the cost of late recognition. The CBC/smear rule can reach 89% sensitivity and 93% specificity. |

# 4. Campylobacter gastroenteritis

Campylobacter cannot be reliably separated clinically from Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, invasive *E. coli*, or other bacterial enteritides. A definitive etiologic diagnosis requires fecal detection. Features that modestly favor Campylobacter are a febrile influenza-like prodrome before diarrhea, intense cramping or right-lower-quadrant pain mimicking appendicitis, prostration, and later bloody diarrhea.

The mean incubation is approximately 3.2 days, with a reasonable range of 1–7 days. Around 30% have a nonspecific fever/headache/myalgia prodrome, and intestinal symptoms can be delayed 2–3 days. Diarrhea is often profuse; after 1–2 diarrheal days, gross blood appears in about 15% overall and 30% of hospitalized patients. Vomiting occurs in only about 15%; improvement usually begins after 3–4 illness days.

Across community outbreaks, reported symptom ranges were broad: diarrhea 52–100%, abdominal pain 56–99%, fever 6–75%, vomiting 1–42%, and visible blood 0.5–32%. These are frequencies among cases, not diagnostic sensitivities, and the overlap precludes a useful pathogen-specific specificity.

**Temporal interpretation:** on day 1, a febrile prodrome can look like influenza; by day 3, watery diarrhea and severe cramps suggest bacterial enteritis but not Campylobacter specifically; by day 5+, blood and persistent abdominal pain support invasive enteritis while symptoms often begin improving. Clinical impression sensitivity/specificity at each time point is **not established**.

Shipboard differentials include norovirus, enterotoxigenic *E. coli*, Salmonella, Shigella, *Vibrio parahaemolyticus*, *C. difficile*, and toxin-mediated food poisoning. Clustering after poultry, unpasteurized milk, contaminated water, or cross-contamination raises the prior probability. Maritime surveillance is constrained because ship specimens often must be tested ashore.

# 5. Legionella pneumonia

## Bedside clues

No single symptom, sign, chemistry value, or radiographic pattern is sufficiently specific. Suspicion rises when pneumonia is accompanied by several of the following:

- high fever with relative bradycardia;
- diarrhea, abdominal symptoms, nausea, or vomiting;
- confusion or other neurological findings;
- hyponatremia or hypophosphatemia;
- acute kidney injury, microscopic hematuria, or rhabdomyolysis/elevated creatine kinase;
- lymphopenia and markedly elevated CRP/ferritin;
- rapid progression from a patchy unilateral infiltrate to bilateral disease; and
- exposure to aerosol-producing water systems, showers, spas/hot tubs, cooling towers, or recently stagnant plumbing.

The combination is more informative than any individual feature, but the literature does not establish sensitivity/specificity for unaided clinical impression. Imaging is not pathognomonic, and radiographs may worsen during the first week despite clinical improvement. (rello2024severelegionnaires’disease pages 4-5)

**Time course:** day 1 often resembles influenza or another CAP; by day 3, pneumonia plus GI/neurologic findings and high fever is more suggestive; by day 5+, hyponatremia, renal injury, rhabdomyolysis, confusion, and rapidly progressive bilateral pneumonia increase suspicion. These later clues improve discrimination but represent more advanced disease.

Published Legionella prediction scores combine fever, dry cough, sodium, LDH, CRP, and platelet count. Because most components require laboratory tests, they do not qualify as a purely bedside impression. Their performance varies by threshold and population; they should guide testing, not replace it.

## Maritime evidence

Legionella has unusually strong ship-specific evidence. A systematic review found that 59/83 ship-associated cases (71.1%) were linked to hot tubs; recurrent events were often associated with inadequate disinfection, stagnation, poor temperature control, or maintenance failures.

Two recent cruise-ship outbreaks involved eight and four confirmed cases and implicated private balcony hot tubs, with some environmental concentrations above 1,000 CFU/mL.

A 2003 cruise-liner outbreak produced eight confirmed cases and four suspected cases; most illness began near the end of the voyage or after return, and four confirmed cases had visited the ship’s physician with severe respiratory illness.

Most instructively, two mechanics exposed while working on a cargo ship’s water pump both died after being repeatedly diagnosed with influenza. Matching *L. pneumophila* was recovered from lung tissue and the ship’s cooling-water circuit. This directly demonstrates poor early clinical distinguishability and the diagnostic importance of water-system work.

Environmental colonization is not equivalent to clinical disease, but it changes the prior probability: one review found Legionella in 23/28 ferries and 91/346 samples, including very high concentrations in some systems.

# 6. Vibrio infections

## Gastroenteritis

After raw or undercooked seafood—especially oysters—Vibrio gastroenteritis produces nausea, vomiting, watery diarrhea, cramps, fever, and chills. This syndrome is not clinically distinguishable from other seafood-associated gastroenteritis. Exposure defines suspicion; unaided sensitivity and specificity are **not established**.

For *V. vulnificus*, ingestion-related illness often begins rapidly: median onset about 18 hours, although reported delays are longer. Uncomplicated gastroenteritis is generally mild and self-limited.

## Wound infection and septicemia

The shipboard-recognizable high-risk syndrome is much more distinctive:

- open wound, puncture, fishhook injury, shellfish handling, or seawater/brackish-water exposure;
- sudden, severe pain and edema out of proportion to initial skin appearance;
- rapidly spreading erythema/cellulitis;
- ecchymoses or hemorrhagic bullae;
- systemic toxicity, hypotension, or confusion; and
- chronic liver disease, hemochromatosis, diabetes, renal disease, or immunosuppression.

Symptoms and clinical outcome may develop within 24 hours. Cellulitis can rapidly acquire hemorrhagic bullae and progress to necrosis or fasciitis; in primary septicemia, secondary skin lesions often appear within 24 hours of systemic symptoms.

These findings are highly alarming but not specific to Vibrio: *Aeromonas*, group A streptococcus, *Clostridium*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, and polymicrobial necrotizing infection remain alternatives. The specific exposure–phenotype combination is therefore the key bedside clue. Physical findings alone are acknowledged to be nonspecific.

**Temporal interpretation:** day 1 may show only pain, erythema, GI symptoms, or fever; by day 3, untreated invasive disease may have bullae, necrosis, shock, or multiorgan failure; waiting until day 5 is unsafe. A 24-hour antibiotic delay has been associated with substantially increased fatality, and a 72-hour delay with extremely poor outcomes.

# Shipboard synthesis

1. **Use clinical findings to initiate precautions, not to certify etiology.** The clearest measured examples are EVD, where broad criteria were sensitive but admitted 72.5% PCR-negative patients, and HPS, where good accuracy required CBC/smear data rather than observation alone.
2. **Exposure history has disproportionate value at sea:** measles contact and immunity; EVD body-fluid/funeral/healthcare exposure; HPS rodent-infested compartments or endemic shore exposure; Legionella aerosolized ship water; Campylobacter poultry/cross-contamination; and Vibrio seafood or seawater wounds.
3. **Clinical specificity often rises only after dangerous delay.** Rash improves measles recognition; GI progression/contact improve EVD triage; pulmonary edema improves HPS recognition; bullae improve Vibrio recognition. By then transmission or physiologic deterioration may already be substantial.
4. **Confined-space evidence is strongest for measles transmission and Legionella exposure.** Ship-associated measles has produced hundreds of secondary cases ashore, while multiple Legionella outbreaks have been linked to hot tubs and water systems.
5. **Maritime diagnostic data are incomplete.** Short voyages and limited infirmary laboratories mean cases may be recognized only after disembarkation; land-based specimen testing delays confirmation and can force contact action on false-positive presumptive diagnoses.

The defensible interpretation of the literature is therefore not that a shipboard clinician can achieve a fixed sensitivity and specificity on day 1, 3, or 5 for every pathogen. Rather, formal quantitative bedside accuracy has been measured only for selected outbreak triage tools, and most apparent improvement over time results from the appearance of later, more severe, or more characteristic findings. Early isolation and exposure-informed suspicion must precede laboratory confirmation.
