# Does the mega outbreak record require a food supply chain?

**Edison evidence readout.** Public acute gastroenteritis (AGE) postings, 2006–2019, at repository revision `74a6eff3ec45734b377acb07afe287ca6298f02f`. The [voyage ledger](mega_voyage_source_evidence.csv) covers all 15 postings assigned to mega by passenger complement in the [cross-hull comparison](cross_hull_outbreak_evidence.md). Fourteen name norovirus alone. Norwegian Joy has unknown etiology.

**The public evidence supports a shared food introduction for three Spirit voyages and a probable traveler introduction followed by onboard spread for one mega voyage.** No source retrieved for the 15 mega postings linked a food item on board to acknowledged contamination. The other 14 have weaker leads or unresolved sources. The availability and depth of public investigations vary, so these counts cannot estimate how often either route occurred.

## A mega voyage with dates of illness onset

CDC's March 29 trip report names *Explorer of the Seas*, the February 26–March 5, 2006 itinerary, and the passenger and crew complements. An early peak prompted investigators to consider a food source. Five passenger surveys recorded illness before boarding. Passenger cases reported a sick social contact more often than controls (odds ratio 5.0, 95% CI 1.5–16.9). Interviews identified no common dining location among ill crew. CDC judged it likely that people ill before boarding propagated disease through person-to-person contact. The surveys sampled cases and controls, so the five preboarding reports cannot be extrapolated to the manifest. Neri et al. describe an unnamed ship B in the same period with the same 3,245 passengers, 1,184 crew and 91 passenger surveys. Counting that study separately would duplicate the investigation. [CDC trip report, pp. 1–3, 7, 10](https://web.archive.org/web/20100309144728id_/http://www.cdc.gov/nceh/vsp/surv/outbreak/2006/explorerreport.pdf) · [Neri et al., Methods and Results](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1708-8305.2008.00199.x)

## How the other mega leads compare

Each CSV row preserves ship, dates, agent, case counts, denominators, source type and provenance. The search combined ship names and sailing dates with outbreak, food, source and investigation terms, followed archived VSP reports, and checked peer-reviewed investigations and contemporaneous accounts against the corresponding voyages. A food attribution requires an exposure linked to the voyage and evidence that the food was contaminated. A clinical specimen identifies the patient's agent. A shared date or news report can identify a lead. Unattributed voyages may have unpublished investigations.

- **Consecutive sailings.** VSP documented three consecutive *Freedom of the Seas* voyages with elevated illness and recommended a 48-hour sailing delay. Two appear in the ledger, including a December posting below 3% in both roles by final counts. The operator suspected introduction by a previously exposed guest on the November 26 sailing and suggested a similar explanation on *Liberty of the Seas* in 2007. The causes of recurrence remain unresolved. [VSP update](https://web.archive.org/web/20070604231403/http://www.cdc.gov/nceh/vsp/surv/outbreak/2006/dec3freedom.htm) · [Freedom operator statement](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/nearly-400-ill-from-cruise-ship-virus/) · [Liberty operator statement](https://www.travelweekly.com/Cruise-Travel/Suspected-Norovirus-outbreak-on-Liberty-of-the-Seas-sailing)
- **A meal and a coincident voyage.** An *Oasis of the Seas* passenger recounted illness after a shore lunch. ABC reported that the outbreak source was unknown. *Conquest* overlapped the first AIDA voyage and exceeded 3% only in crew (35/1,158 crew, 17/3,241 passengers). Its update supplies no common item or shipment. [Oasis account](https://abcnews.com/US/270-cruise-ship-passengers-sickened-norovirus-royal-caribbean/story?id=60291868) · [Conquest update](https://web.archive.org/web/20200713203129/https://www.cdc.gov/nceh/vsp/surv/outbreak/2019/Conquest_9-7.html)
- **Partial timing.** The VSP narratives date notifications of elevated illness to four elapsed days after *Mariner of the Seas* departed in July 2006 and five after *Explorer* departed in April. These constrain when illness was recognized. The April *Explorer* posting concerns a different sailing from the February investigation. Neither narrative supplies an onset curve or public posting date. [Mariner update](https://web.archive.org/web/20071120051438/http://www.cdc.gov/nceh/vsp/surv/outbreak/2006/update_july31marineroftheseas.htm) · [April Explorer update](https://web.archive.org/web/20070615124804/http://www.cdc.gov/nceh/vsp/surv/outbreak/2006/update_april30exploreroftheseas.htm)

## The traced Spirit cluster and the cross-hull medians

Rispens et al. describe three U.S.-jurisdiction voyages on two anonymized ships. The dates, investigation visits and passenger/crew case pairs (117/8, 85/10, 83/10) match *AIDAdiva* September 5–23, *AIDAluna* September 28–October 12, and *AIDAdiva* October 3–13, 2019. The first passenger denominator differs (2,046 in MMWR, 2,055 on the posting). The hosted index also gives November end dates for both *AIDAdiva* rows. Their original voyage updates agree with the investigation chronology. All three fall in Spirit under either source. Questionnaires, shared item lot numbers, positive food and clinical specimens, and cessation after withdrawal support a common frozen-fruit source at the cluster level. These matches do not identify the ten European ships also mentioned in MMWR. ## What the mechanistic model needs to explain

For *Explorer*, the onset curve tests whether the model reproduces the two-day lag between passenger and crew peaks. CDC considered later crew illness compatible with both shared food exposure and person-to-person spread. Its judgment about introduction drew on preboarding illness, contact histories and the dining evidence. A model can match the final passenger AR and still get the timing wrong. For fleet comparisons, include voyages with no posting, apply role-specific reporting and the either-role posting rule, and account for voyage duration. The estimate of roughly 5.1 public AGE postings per 1,000 eligible voyage reports pools causes and hulls. The available class denominators use tonnage. The 15 postings selected by passenger complement lack a corresponding all-voyage denominator. [Cross-hull frequency and timing](cross_hull_outbreak_evidence.md#posting-frequency-and-timing)

For the AIDA cluster, a model explaining the dependence between the two ships needs to allow a common introduction across voyages. The documented exposure itself can represent that event. Removing the cluster barely changes the Spirit role medians. The archived mega solver screen had one posting among 16 selected seeds under each of two paired solvers, on the same seed. That screen cannot establish a fleet rate or whether the model can reproduce the aggregate record without detailed supply structure. [Archived comparison](cross_hull_outbreak_evidence.md#what-the-archived-model-contributes)

**Decision for Edison.** Treat shared food introductions as an evidenced explanation for particular clusters. The public record gives no basis to require explicit supplier, shipment, or menu structure for the aggregate mega pattern. Evaluate the existing model against illness timing by role where measured and against posting frequency and role splits under comparable surveillance definitions. The evidence that would most change this conclusion is residual clustering across ships associated with independently documented shared deliveries, including comparable unaffected voyages.
